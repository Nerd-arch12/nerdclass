Reading 1

Health and eating schedules

Your Weekend Eating Schedule May Affect Your Heart

On weekdays, breakfast might be at 8 a.m. and dinner at 7 p.m. But on weekends, many of us like to sleep a little longer and take the day at a slower pace. So breakfast often turns into brunch, dinner gets pushed back, and late-night snacks may find their way onto the menu.

That may not sound like a big deal. But according to new research, eating at different times on weekdays and weekends — what researchers call “eating jetlag” — could affect heart health, especially in men.

The research, published in Communications Medicine, looked at more than 100,000 adults in France. Participants recorded what they ate and drank, as well as the time they had their first and last food or drink of the day. Researchers then looked at these people's “eating jetlag” and followed their health for an average of eight years.

Among men, a bigger difference between weekday and weekend eating times was linked to a higher risk of heart disease. The link remained even after the researchers looked at things like diet, when the men went to sleep and how long they slept.

It also didn't matter much whether these men ate earlier or later on weekends. Every extra hour of eating jetlag — forward or back — was linked to a 14% higher risk of cardiovascular disease and a 21% higher risk of coronary heart disease.

However, the same link was not found in women. The researchers say this could be because of biological differences between men and women, as well as differences in lifestyle and daily routines.

So, sleeping in and having a late breakfast may be one of the best parts of the weekend. But especially if you're a man, your heart may be happier if your meal times don't move around too much!

Reading 2

Music and relationships

Couples Share Musical Taste More than Personality

Liking the same music as your partner could mean more than just making it easier to choose what to listen to on a road trip. In fact, it might be a big reason why you're together!

That's because research published in Social Psychology and Personality Science found that people in romantic relationships tend to share more musical tastes than personality traits with their partner.

The research was comprised of three studies. The participants in each study were all adults in romantic relationships, and could only take part if their partner took part too. Their average relationship length ranged from three-and-a-half to seven years.

In each study, the participants answered questionnaires about their taste in music, as well as their personality and how they felt about their relationship.

The first study included 99 couples who were aged 18 to 39, and compared couples both to each other and to other random people in the group.

It was found that couples shared many more musical likes and dislikes with each other than they did with random group members. In fact, couples shared more musical tastes than personality traits!

Having similar personalities didn't make couples any happier with their relationship, either — but having a shared favorite musician was linked with slightly higher feelings of tenderness.

Almost the exact same results were found in the second study, which included another 99 couples, this time aged up to 86 years old.

The third study included 111 couples aged 18 to 78, and people in this group were asked if they were musicians themselves.

Once again, couples tended to share taste in music — and this wasn't affected by whether or not they were musicians. Interestingly though, this group reported slightly stronger feelings of love if they shared a most disliked musician.

So if you're looking for a partner, maybe instead of going to bars or using dating apps, you should try going to a concert!

Reading 3

Workplace identity

67% of Americans Change Their Personality at Work

Have you ever changed your personality to fit in better at work? If so, you are far from alone.

US staffing company Express Employment Professionals surveyed 1,000 US job seekers, including those who already have a job but want a new one.

It found that 67% of these job seekers have changed some part of their personality for work — and 63% say that who they are at work is very different from who they are at home.

However, the older people are, the less they say their work personality is different. While 80% of the youngest job-seekers say they've changed part of their personality to fit in at work, only 50% of the oldest job-seekers say they have.

Express Employment Professionals said this challenges the common belief that younger employees are less willing to change how they act to fit in at work.

The survey also found that 98% of job seekers think adjusting your personality to workplace culture is important — with 86% calling being able to do so an essential professional skill. Eighty-three percent even said that employees who either can't or won't adapt may not stay with a company for long.

Express Employment Professionals also did a separate survey of around 1,000 hiring decision-makers at US companies, and they agreed with the importance of personality. Ninety-four percent said that someone's personality affects perceptions of their performance, and 99% agreed that a positive or approachable personality can accelerate career advancement.

However, not everyone agrees that personality should have so much power. More than half of job seekers — and 71% of the youngest ones — said their personality has nothing to do with how well they do their job.

However, just 36% of the oldest job-seekers said personality doesn't affect how well they can do their job.

Advanced Grammar

Explanations, examples and practice

Hedging

Academic writers use may, might, could, appears to, suggests that and is associated with to avoid claims stronger than the evidence.

Irregular eating causes heart disease. → Irregular eating may be associated with a higher risk of heart disease.

Passive reporting

Use It is believed that... or subject + is believed to + verb to emphasize information.

Researchers believe music influences relationships. → Music is believed to influence relationships.

Concession

Use although + clause, despite + noun/-ing, or nevertheless + sentence.

Although the sample was large, the study had limitations.

Inversion

After an initial negative expression, place the auxiliary before the subject.

The study did not prove causation at any point. → At no point did the study prove causation.

Mixed conditionals

Connect an unreal past condition to a present result: If + past perfect, would + base verb.

If the company had invested in cooling, its costs would be lower now.
